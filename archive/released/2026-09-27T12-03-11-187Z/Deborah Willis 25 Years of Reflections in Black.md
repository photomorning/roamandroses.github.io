# Deborah Willis: 25 Years of Reflections in Black

Deborah Willis is a photographer, curator, historian and educator, and the description that fits her best is the one she is often given: an excavator, unearthing a visual testimony of Black life. Her landmark book *Reflections in Black*, which marked its twenty-fifth anniversary, changed how the history of photography is understood by recovering the work of Black photographers who had been excluded from the canon.

The distinction matters. Willis did not argue that such photographers ought to have been included. She demonstrated that they had been there the whole time, waiting to be seen.

## The Archive as Activism

Willis's career is a demonstration that curating and archiving are creative acts in their own right, as substantial as making photographs.

By recovering the work of generations of Black photographers, from nineteenth-century studio portraitists through photojournalists to contemporary fine artists, she expanded not only what is known about photographic history but what photography is understood to be capable of. That is a different kind of contribution from taking pictures, and it is arguably rarer.

Her book *Reflections in Black*, published in 2000 and still in circulation a quarter of a century later, gathered images and research that had been dispersed, undocumented or simply unregarded. *Posing Beauty*, which followed in 2009, examined how African Americans have been represented and have represented themselves across more than a century of images. Both projects share a method: find the photographs, establish who made them, and place them where they can be seen.

The activism is in the method rather than in a slogan. An archive that nobody can consult does not change anything. A canon that nobody has written down is not a canon, and the labour of documenting it is what makes it real.

## Why the Archive Matters

For photographers, the practical argument for this kind of work is straightforward.

**Canons are made, not discovered.** The photographers who are taught are the ones whose work was collected, catalogued, published and exhibited. Every omission in that chain produces a permanent absence, and every link restored produces a permanent presence.

**Institutional validation follows evidence.** Willis made the case so thoroughly that institutions had to respond to it. Research, books and exhibitions are the mechanism by which a body of work becomes impossible to ignore.

**The work exists in private collections.** Much of what was recovered had survived in family albums, attics and boxes rather than in institutions, which is why oral history and community relationships are part of archival practice.

**Representation shapes production.** Photographers make work in relation to what they have seen. Expanding the visible record changes what the next generation makes, which is the long-term effect of recovering a lost image-maker.

## Building the Canon You Want to See

Willis's career contains a lesson that generalises beyond any single community: if the existing canon does not include your people and your stories, build a new one.

The steps are unglamorous and available to anyone with curiosity and time.

**Research systematically.** Identify photographers working in the period or place you care about, and check institutional records, local newspapers, church archives and family collections. Most of them are named somewhere.

**Talk to families and communities.** The surviving images are usually with the people who knew the photographer, and they are found by asking rather than by searching catalogues.

**Write it down.** Publishing is what converts research into a record. A blog post, a zine, a small book: anything that can be cited and found by the next researcher.

**Put the work on a wall.** Curate something, at any scale. A community hall exhibition with proper labels does the same work as a museum show.

**Support institutions that take this seriously.** Buy the books, attend the openings, and pressure the programming. Attention is the currency that decides what gets researched next.

## What Photographers Take From This

Willis's own photographs and her scholarly work share a set of habits that any photographer can borrow.

**Treat your own archive as a collection to be curated, not a folder to be filled.** Naming, dating and organising your images makes them usable later, by you and by anyone else.

**Document the context as well as the picture.** Who is this, where were they, why does it matter? A photograph with no information becomes anonymous within a generation.

**Photograph the people around you properly.** The studio portraitists Willis recovered were photographing their own communities, and their work is now historical testimony as well as art.

**Consider the long term.** Work made for a magazine in 1990 is often historically worthless now, while work made for the community it depicts has increased in value. That is worth weighing when deciding what to do next.

**Collaborate with historians.** Photographers and researchers produce more together than either does alone, and the archives that survive are the ones somebody catalogued.

## Practical Tips

- Treat curating and archiving as creative acts as significant as making images.
- Understand that the history of photography is incomplete, and that your work can help complete it.
- Do not wait for institutions to validate your community's contributions.
- Build alternative canons. It is a form of activism, and it works through evidence rather than complaint.
- Date, name and organise your own archive so that it remains usable.
- Record context for your images: names, places, dates and reasons.
- Photograph your own community with the seriousness that studio portraitists once applied to theirs.
- Support and cite the research you want to see continued.

## Conclusion

Deborah Willis's quarter-century of work on *Reflections in Black* is proof that the historical record is not fixed. It is assembled, and the assembly can be contested by anyone willing to do the labour of finding what was left out.

The lesson for photographers is double: the work you make in and about your community has historical value that commercial work usually lacks, and the history of your medium is a subject available to anyone with the patience to research it. Both kinds of work are acts of construction rather than discovery, which is exactly why they last.

## FAQ

### Why is Deborah Willis important to photographic history?

She transformed the understanding of that history by recovering and celebrating the work of Black photographers excluded from the canon. *Reflections in Black* created space for generations of overlooked image-makers, and her curatorial and scholarly work continues to expand what the field includes.

### How can I help broaden photographic history?

Research photographers from communities left out of the mainstream canon, share their work, write about them, curate exhibitions, and support institutions that prioritise representation. The work of recovery is ongoing and mostly unglamorous.

### Is archiving really creative work?

Yes. Selecting, researching, sequencing and contextualising images creates meaning that did not previously exist, and without it a body of work is invisible regardless of its quality.

### What is Reflections in Black about?

It is a history of Black photographers in the United States from 1840 onward, documenting studio portraitists, photojournalists and artists whose work had been largely absent from the standard photographic histories.

### What does Posing Beauty cover?

It examines the representation of African American beauty across more than a century, considering both how Black subjects have been photographed by others and how Black photographers have represented themselves.

### How do I start researching local photographic history?

Begin with families, community organisations, church archives and local newspapers, which hold names and dates that institutional catalogues often lack. Then publish what you find somewhere citable so the next researcher can build on it.
