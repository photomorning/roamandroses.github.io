# Clifford Prince King: Daydreams Transformed Into Photographs

Clifford Prince King says his images begin as daydreams. "Or, experiences that I've had and thought about," he explains. In his exhibition *Hush-a-Bye Dreams*, those daydreams arrive as tender, intimate photographs of Black queer life, images that read less like documentation and more like memory made visible.

That distinction is the whole of his method. King does not go looking for subjects. He begins with something internal, a feeling or a remembered scene, and then works out how to build it in front of a camera.

## The Photograph as Dream

King starts inside and works outward. That order inverts the usual practice, where a photographer finds a subject and then decides what to say about it. His images are constructed from a memory or a half-formed picture in his mind, and the photograph is the process of bringing that into the world.

The result is photography as manifestation rather than observation. For anyone who feels constrained by the assumption that photographs must document something that already exists, the position is genuinely liberating: a camera can build a world as easily as it can record one.

The practical consequence is that the subject becomes a collaboration. If the image began as a daydream, the people in it are participating in its realisation rather than being observed, and the difference shows in their faces. This is why King's work looks arranged and unguarded simultaneously: the situation is constructed, and the people in it are not performing.

## Intimacy as Method

The intimacy in these photographs is a product of relationships rather than direction. King photographs friends, lovers and people from his community, and the closeness in the images cannot be manufactured by technique.

This is worth stating plainly because it is the most transferable thing about his work and the hardest to fake. A photographer who wants pictures like these does not need better lighting. They need people who trust them, and that takes time rather than skill. Trust is the actual instrument.

There is a second implication about subject matter. If intimacy comes from relationships, then the honest subject for any photographer is the life they are actually living: their friends, their family, their neighbourhood, the rooms they occupy. That is less glamorous than a location shoot and produces work that holds attention because something real is visible in it.

## Building Intimate Images

The technique around the trust is what determines whether the photographs work, and King's approach suggests several habits.

**Use domestic space.** Homes carry evidence of how people live, and a familiar room produces a different register from a studio. The furniture, the light and the objects all participate.

**Work with soft, available light.** Window light and lamps produce the dreamlike quality; hard flash does not. Much of the intimacy in these images is simply the quality of the light in a room in the late afternoon.

**Allow ambiguity.** The photographs do not explain themselves, and they are better for it. A viewer who cannot tell whether a scene is affectionate or sad will look longer than one who has been told.

**Photograph the body without objectifying it.** Rest, sleep, contact, undress: the physical intimacy in the work is domestic rather than eroticised, which is what makes it readable as tenderness rather than display.

**Let the frame be close.** A crop that includes a wall and a shoulder rather than a whole figure makes the viewer feel present in the room.

**Work repeatedly with the same people.** The second, third and tenth session with the same subject produces something a first session cannot, and King's archive is built on exactly that.

## World-Building With a Camera

The broader lesson in King's practice is that photography is not obliged to document. It can be fiction, and it can be fiction built from real material.

The method for that is worth extracting:

**Start from an image in your head.** Not a concept: a picture. A light, a position, a mood. Then work out what would have to be true for that picture to exist.

**Cast rather than recruit.** Choose the people who belong in the image because of who they are to you, not because they look right.

**Build the scene minimally.** A room, a piece of light and two people is enough. Elaborate sets tend to swamp the intimacy that is doing the work.

**Shoot what actually happens in the built scene.** Once the situation exists, the photographs are found rather than directed, and the balance between construction and accident is what makes the result feel true.

**Do not resolve the image.** A dream does not explain itself. Keep the frame open and let the viewer complete it.

## Practical Tips

- Start with internal images, daydreams, memories and feelings, rather than external subjects.
- Photograph people you genuinely love and trust.
- Treat the camera as a tool for world-building rather than only for documentation.
- Remember that intimacy is earned through relationships, not manufactured through technique.
- Use soft, available light and domestic spaces.
- Embrace ambiguity. An image that explains itself is finished too quickly.
- Photograph the same people repeatedly. Depth accumulates across sessions.
- Keep the frame close enough that the viewer feels in the room.

## Conclusion

Clifford Prince King's photographs are made the way memories are: from the inside out, in domestic light, with people who are trusted rather than hired. The dreamlike quality in the work is not an effect applied afterwards. It is the natural result of building a scene from something you can already see in your head and then letting it happen.

The lesson is available to any photographer, and it does not require equipment. Photograph the people you love, in the rooms you actually occupy, in the light that is already there. The intimacy that makes the work memorable is a property of the relationships, and the only way to obtain it is to have them.

## FAQ

### How does Clifford Prince King describe his creative process?

He says his images start as daydreams, or as experiences he has had and thought about. Rather than searching for subjects, he begins with an internal image and works to bring it into existence through photography.

### How can I make my photographs feel more dreamlike?

Start with internal imagery rather than external observation, use soft or unusual light, embrace ambiguity, and photograph what feels true rather than what looks impressive. The atmosphere comes from the light and the framing rather than from post-processing.

### Why does intimacy matter so much in his work?

Because it cannot be faked. King photographs people he genuinely loves, which produces a closeness no amount of direction can manufacture. The camera is secondary to the relationship.

### Do I need a studio to work this way?

No. Domestic interiors and available light are the conditions the work depends on. A studio would remove most of what makes the images feel intimate.

### What if I do not have a community to photograph?

Build one, or start with what you have: family, flatmates, close friends. The relevant question is not how many people you know but whether the people in the photographs trust you.

### Is constructed photography still documentary?

It is neither easily. King's images are built situations containing real emotion, which puts them somewhere between fiction and record. That position is a long tradition in photography and a legitimate one.
